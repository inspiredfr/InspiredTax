## The Question That Arrives Around R1 Million

Somewhere past the point where freelancing becomes a serious practice, the incorporation question appears — usually via a friend's accountant: "you're mad to be paying personal rates." Sometimes correct. Often not. The honest comparison has more moving parts than a rate table, so let's lay them out properly.

## The Sole Proprietor Baseline

Trading in your own name, your profit is simply your taxable income: personal tax table, s11(a) deductions, s11F retirement contributions, home office, the lot. One tax return, one provisional cycle, no separation between the business's money and yours.

Strengths: simplicity, every deduction this site writes about, direct access to your own cash, and losses (in genuine loss years) offsetting other income subject to ring-fencing rules. Weakness: at higher incomes, the upper personal marginal rates bite on every additional rand, and there's no legal separation between business liabilities and your personal assets.

## The Company: Two Layers, Not One Rate

A private company pays **27%** on its taxable profits. That number, sitting next to a top personal marginal rate, is the entire sales pitch — and it's misleading until you add the second layer: the company's money is not your money.

Getting profit into your hands takes one of two taxed routes:

- **Salary** — deductible for the company, taxed in your hands at personal rates via PAYE. Net effect: roughly where you started as a sole proprietor.
- **Dividends** — paid from after-tax profit, then hit with **20% dividends tax**. Combined: 27% plus 20% of the remaining 73% ≈ **41.6%** effective on fully distributed profits.

So a company that earns and immediately distributes everything saves little or nothing. The genuine tax advantages live elsewhere:

1. **Retention.** Profit left in the company is taxed at 27% and stops there — for now. If you're reinvesting in the business or building a war chest, the deferral of the second layer is real value.
2. **SBC relief.** A qualifying Small Business Corporation under section 12E pays graduated rates — a zero band, then reduced rates before 27% kicks in — plus accelerated write-offs on assets. For qualifying companies this is substantial. But the gatekeeping matters: s12E excludes companies where more than a fixed portion of income is investment income or from "personal services" *unless* the company maintains at least three full-time unconnected employees in its core operations. A one-person consultancy typically fails that test. Verify against the current s12E requirements before assuming the rates apply to you.
3. **Income smoothing.** Salary set at a sensible level, topped with dividends in strong years, lets you manage which brackets your personal income lands in.

## The Trap Written Specifically for Freelancers

If your company earns most of its income from one client, and you'd look like that client's employee but for the company in between, the **personal service provider** rules can apply: the client must withhold employees' tax from payments to your company, and the company's deductions are severely restricted. The rules exist precisely to stop one-client contractors incorporating their way out of employee taxation. If your practice is one anchor client and a laptop, incorporation may buy you the worst of both worlds — take specific advice before creating that structure.

## The Costs Nobody Prices In

- **Compliance stack:** CIPC annual returns, a company ITR14, the company's own provisional tax cycle, PAYE registration and monthly EMP201s if you draw salary, possibly an accounting officer or auditor depending on your public interest score. Realistically this means paying an accountant from day one.
- **Discipline:** the company's bank account is not your wallet. Drawings booked to a loan account create debit loans with their own tax consequences (deemed dividends). Sloppy separation unwinds the whole point of the structure.
- **Lost simplicity on the way out:** deregistering or unwinding a company later — with assets in it — has tax consequences of its own.

Against that, the non-tax benefit is real: **limited liability**. A company between your contracts and your house matters more in some trades (construction, anything with professional negligence exposure beyond what insurance covers) than others. For many knowledge workers, professional indemnity insurance answers the risk question more cheaply than an entity does.

## A Sober Decision Framework

Incorporation earns its keep when several of these are true: profits comfortably exceed what you need to live on (retention value); multiple clients and, ideally, people beyond yourself (SBC eligibility and PSP safety); genuine liability exposure; plans to build something sellable rather than a personal practice; and tolerance for paying an accountant to keep the machinery compliant.

Stay a sole proprietor while: you spend most of what you earn (the second layer erases the saving); you're effectively one client's contractor (PSP risk); or the admin premium exceeds the modelled tax saving — run the actual numbers for your income, not the anecdote from the braai.

And remember what doesn't change either way: provisional tax. The IRP6 rhythm this site is built around continues — as your personal obligation, the company's, or both. The February article series on estimates applies to whichever taxpayer you become.

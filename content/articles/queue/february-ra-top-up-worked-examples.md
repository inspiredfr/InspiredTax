FAQ: How much tax does a R100,000 RA contribution save?|Roughly your marginal rate times the contribution, provided you stay within the s11F limit — for a freelancer in the 39% bracket, about R39,000 back at assessment. The saving is real but partial: the contribution still costs you the balance in locked-away cash, which is why it should be savings you intended to make anyway.
FAQ: What happens if I contribute more than the s11F limit allows?|Nothing is lost. The excess carries forward and is treated as a contribution in following years, deductible within those years' limits, and any amount still undeducted at retirement reduces the tax on your retirement benefits. Overshooting is inefficient, not fatal.
FAQ: What is the actual deadline for a February RA contribution?|The fund must receive and allocate the money within the tax year ending 28 February 2027 — a Sunday. Funds publish annual cut-offs, typically mid-February, for lump sums to be processed in time. Miss the fund's cut-off and the deduction shifts to 2027/28.

## Lump-Sum Season, By the Numbers

October's article on this site made the planning case: compute your s11F ceiling early, decide the funding pattern, diarise the cut-off. Now it's execution season — and execution decisions go better with actual numbers than with principles. So: three freelancers, three top-ups, and what each is really worth. (Brackets and rebates per the 2026/27 tables; your exact figures will differ slightly — the structure won't.)

First, the rule being worked: contributions to retirement funds deduct up to **27.5% of the greater of remuneration or taxable income** (measured before this deduction, including taxable capital gains), **capped at R430,000** per year. Freelancers measure against taxable income; the deduction reduces it; the tax saved is the deduction times the marginal rates it displaces.

## Example One: The Mid-Career Designer — R450,000, R50,000 Top-Up

Thandi projects R450,000 of taxable income before retirement contributions. Her R3,000 monthly debit order has contributed R36,000. Her ceiling: 27.5% × R450,000 = **R123,750** — she has R87,750 of unused room.

She can spare R50,000 without touching her tax reserve. At R450,000 she sits in the 36% bracket, and the R50,000 deduction lands entirely within it: roughly **R18,000 of tax saved**. Taxable income falls to R364,000; her February IRP6 estimate uses the lower figure, so part of the benefit arrives immediately as a smaller period-2 payment rather than waiting for assessment.

Cost-benefit stated honestly: she's converted R50,000 of cash into retirement capital plus R18,000 of tax relief. The R32,000 net is invested, compounding untaxed inside the fund — but locked until at least 55. For money she was saving anyway, the deduction is a 36% head start no discretionary investment matches.

## Example Two: The Consultant at the Cap's Edge — R950,000, R100,000 Top-Up

Sipho's taxable income before contributions is R950,000; monthly contributions total R60,000. Ceiling: 27.5% × R950,000 = **R261,250**. Room: R201,250.

A R100,000 lump sum deducts fully. At his level the rand displaced sit in the 41% bracket: about **R41,000 saved**, taxable income down to R790,000. Two knock-on effects worth noticing:

- His February estimate drops meaningfully — the top-up is part of an honest period-2 calculation *provided the contribution is actually made* before year-end. Claiming a planned-but-unexecuted contribution in the estimate is how an RA strategy becomes a paragraph 20 problem.
- He's still nowhere near the R430,000 cap — the binding constraint at his level is the 27.5%, and that's what the October planning exercise establishes each year.

## Example Three: The Windfall Year — R1.6 Million Including a Capital Gain

Nadia's practice earned R1.1 million, and a share disposal added a taxable capital gain, putting taxable income before contributions at R1.6 million. Two things expand simultaneously: her ceiling — 27.5% × R1.6m = **R440,000, clipped to the R430,000 cap** — and her February risk, since her income is approaching the R1.8 million line where estimate accuracy rules harden.

She has contributed R120,000 through the year and executes a **R250,000 lump sum** — large, but this is precisely the year for it: the deduction displaces rand taxed at the top of her scale (roughly **R100,000 saved** at 41–45%), and dropping taxable income to R1.23 million moves her February estimate to a safer distance from the R1.8m threshold's 80%-of-actual regime. The windfall funds its own shelter.

Had she contributed R300,000 instead — overshooting her remaining room — the excess would simply **carry forward**: treated as contributed next year, deductible within 2027/28's limit, and ultimately set against retirement benefits if never absorbed. Inefficient timing, zero permanent loss.

## Execution: The Part That Actually Fails

The arithmetic above fails in practice for exactly one reason: money arriving late. The contribution counts for 2026/27 only if the fund **receives and allocates it within the year** — and with 28 February 2027 on a Sunday, plus fund processing times, the real-world deadlines are the funds' published cut-offs, typically **mid-February**. EFT clearance, new-policy underwriting, or a section 14 transfer in progress can all add days.

The clean sequence: this week, confirm your projected taxable income and remaining s11F room; next week, instruct the lump sum and get written confirmation of the fund's cut-off; mid-February, confirm receipt and allocation in writing; then reflect the contribution in the February IRP6 you submit in the final fortnight. Keep the contribution certificate with the estimate working paper — the two documents that make both the deduction and the estimate defensible.

One discipline above all: the top-up competes with the February tax payment for the same pool of cash. Fund the IRP6 first — the RA deduction saves you 36–45 cents on the rand, but a short-paid IRP6 costs 10% penalties plus interest on the whole shortfall. Shelter with the surplus, never with SARS's money.

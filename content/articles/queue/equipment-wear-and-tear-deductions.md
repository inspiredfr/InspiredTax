## Equipment Is a Deduction on a Timer

The tools of a freelance trade — laptop, camera gear, monitors, office furniture, a serious desk chair — are deductible, but not the way a software subscription is. Assets with a useful life beyond a year can't be expensed outright; instead section 11(e) grants an annual **wear-and-tear allowance** spreading the cost over the asset's expected life. Understanding the mechanics turns the December–February window into a planning opportunity — and stops you overestimating what a year-end shopping trip will do to your tax bill.

## The R7,000 Rule: The Freelancer's Best Friend

Assets costing **R7,000 or less** may be written off **in full in the year of purchase**. This covers a remarkable share of a knowledge worker's real spending: a monitor, a phone (many models), a printer, a desk, software licences bought outright, hard drives, microphones, most chairs.

Each qualifying item is judged on its own cost, but with a boundary: items that function only as part of a set are treated together. A camera body and its dedicated lens kit bought as a working unit shouldn't be artificially split into sub-R7,000 pieces to dodge the threshold. Genuine standalone items, though, each get their own R7,000 test — buying a monitor in November and another in January is two immediate write-offs, no apportionment, no schedule.

## Above R7,000: The Write-Off Periods

Larger assets are written off straight-line over the periods SARS accepts (per its published schedule in Interpretation Note 47). The ones freelancers actually meet:

- **Computers and laptops — 3 years.** A R36,000 machine gives a R12,000 annual allowance.
- **Cellphones and tablets — typically 2–3 years** depending on classification.
- **Office furniture — 6 years.**
- **Cameras and specialised equipment — commonly 5–6 years**, category-dependent.
- **Passenger vehicles — 5 years**, with the vehicle claim wrapped into the travel deduction rules covered in our vehicle expenses guide.

Two adjustments apply on top:

1. **Part-year apportionment.** Acquire an asset mid-year and the first year's allowance is pro-rated for the months held. This is the fact that deflates the "buy it in February for the deduction" idea — a laptop bought 1 February 2027 earns one-twelfth of its first-year allowance in 2026/27.
2. **Business-use apportionment.** Mixed-use assets support only the business share of the allowance. Be realistic and consistent; a 100% claim on the household's only computer invites exactly the question it appears to.

Keep an asset register — a simple sheet with each asset's description, date, cost, write-off period, business percentage, and remaining value. It takes minutes, and it's the first document requested when equipment claims are verified. Keep purchase invoices for the asset's life plus five years.

## Selling or Scrapping: The Exit Rules

The allowance system reconciles when an asset leaves:

- Sell above tax value and the excess claimed back is **recouped** — added back to income (up to original cost; anything above cost enters CGT territory).
- Scrap or sell below tax value and s11(o) may allow the shortfall as a final deduction, subject to its requirements.

Practically: when you sell your old laptop to fund the new one, that R4,000 isn't tax-free — it interacts with what you've claimed. Record it in the register and let the reconciliation happen properly.

## So When Does the Pre-Year-End Purchase Make Sense?

Strip away the folklore and the true cases are:

- **You genuinely need the asset now.** The business case leads; the allowance follows. A tool that earns income from December beats one bought in March purely because the calendar turned.
- **It's under R7,000.** The immediate write-off is real money this year, and a February purchase of a small asset still deducts in full — the R7,000 rule isn't apportioned the way the allowance is.
- **Cash flow smoothing.** In a strong year, replacing small equipment you'd otherwise buy in April pulls a full deduction into the high-income year. Legitimate, modest, and only for items you'd buy anyway.

And the false case, stated plainly: spending R30,000 on deferred-schedule equipment in late February to "reduce tax" converts R30,000 of cash into roughly R800 of first-year allowance. The maths never supports buying things for the deduction; it supports timing things you already need.

## The February List to Start Now

Late November is the moment to write down: what equipment the practice actually needs in the next six months, what each item costs against the R7,000 line, and what your projected taxable income looks like. Then place the genuinely-needed purchases where they serve both the work and the year — and update the asset register the day each one arrives, while the invoice is still in your inbox.

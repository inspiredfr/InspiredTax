## The Season of Generous Intentions and Grey Areas

Every December, freelancers do the rounds: a case of wine to the anchor client, lunch for the agency team that feeds them work, branded notebooks for the smaller accounts. Then February's bookkeeping asks the awkward question — is any of this deductible?

The answer isn't in a "gifts" section of the Income Tax Act, because there isn't one for this. Everything runs through the general deduction formula in **section 11(a)**: expenditure actually incurred **in the production of income**, not of a capital nature — filtered by s23(g), which strikes out amounts not laid out for trade purposes. Employees face a specific prohibition on entertainment claims via s23(m); independent traders don't, which is why this deduction genuinely exists for freelancers while remaining folklore for salaried friends.

## The Production-of-Income Test, Honestly Applied

The question SARS asks — and you should ask first — is what the spending was *for*. Client goodwill spending passes when it's plausibly part of how your trade earns:

- **A year-end gift to an active client** whose repeat work is part of your income base: defensible. The relationship is a revenue asset; modest maintenance of it is a trade expense.
- **Lunch to pitch or scope a project**: defensible — the link to income is direct.
- **A gift to your biggest client that happens to be your brother-in-law**: the personal relationship contaminates the trade purpose. Mixed motives usually resolve against the taxpayer.
- **Hospitality vastly out of proportion to the client's value** — box seats for a client worth one small invoice a year — invites the s23(g) question of whether this was really trade expenditure.

Two principles keep claims on the right side. **Proportionality:** spending should look sensible next to the income the relationship produces. **Specificity:** you should be able to say, per item, which client and what business purpose. "Marketing — various" is where these claims go to die in a verification.

## Gifts vs Marketing: A Useful Distinction

Branded items distributed broadly — notebooks, calendars, merch with your logo, samples of your product — are closer to **advertising** than gifting: promotional expenditure with a clear trade character and generally the most comfortable claim in this whole area. A named gift to a specific individual is the s11(a)-with-scrutiny territory above. When December spending can honestly take the promotional shape, its tax position improves.

## The VAT Trap for Registered Freelancers

If you're a VAT vendor, note the sharp rule: the VAT Act **denies input tax on "entertainment"** — broadly the provision of food, beverages, accommodation, hospitality. The client lunch may be deductible for income tax while its VAT is unrecoverable. Book it VAT-inclusive as an expense and don't claim the input. Genuinely promotional goods (trading stock given away, branded materials) fall outside the entertainment denial, though giving away stock has its own output-VAT considerations. If your December generosity is material and you're VAT-registered, this is a fifteen-minute conversation with your accountant that prevents a recurring return error.

## What the Record Looks Like

The whole area is evidence-driven because motive is the issue. For each claim, keep:

- The invoice or till slip;
- **Who** — the client or contact it went to;
- **Why** — one line: "year-end gift, retained client since 2024", "lunch — scoping Q1 retainer renewal";
- For events or meals, who attended.

Captured at the time in your bookkeeping notes, this takes seconds and converts an arguable claim into a documented one. Reconstructed in July, it's guesswork with a burden of proof attached — and the burden is yours.

## A December Rule of Thumb

Deduct with confidence: modest gifts to genuine, active clients; business-purposed meals; broadly distributed branded and promotional items — all recorded with who and why. Think twice: anything lavish, anything to friends and family who are nominally clients, anything you'd struggle to explain across a desk at a verification. And leave out entirely: your own festive entertaining, staffless "staff parties" for one, and gifts whose honest motive is affection rather than income.

Generosity in December is good business. Just let the paperwork be as thoughtful as the gesture — the deduction is real, but it belongs to the freelancers who can show their reasoning.

## Why December Breaks Freelance Finances

Every experienced freelancer knows the December shape: corporate clients shut down mid-month, invoices sent in November drift into January approval cycles, and personal spending peaks at precisely the moment income dips. It's a survivable squeeze — except that the calendar hides a second act. Twelve weeks after the office parties, the **February provisional payment** falls due, and it's typically the larger of the two: the payment that trues up the whole year at near-actual figures.

The freelancers who have a brutal February are almost never the ones who earned too little. They're the ones whose tax money got absorbed into December.

## The Accrual Detail That Makes It Worse

Here's the mechanism that surprises people: for trade income, tax generally follows **accrual** — the point you become entitled to the money — not receipt. The invoice you issued in December for completed work belongs in the 2026/27 year even if the client only pays you in February.

Consequences:

- A wave of slow-paying clients compresses your cash while leaving your February estimate untouched. You owe tax on income you haven't received yet.
- Deliberately delaying invoicing for finished work to push income into next year doesn't reliably work either, since entitlement — not the invoice date — drives accrual, and manufactured deferral around year-end draws exactly the scrutiny you'd expect.

The lesson isn't a clever timing trick. It's that your tax reserve has to be built from the income as it's earned, because the liability crystallises on that schedule whether or not the cash cooperates.

## The Set-Aside System

The fix is old, boring, and works. Three rules:

### 1. A separate account, fed automatically

Open a dedicated savings account — at minimum a separate space you never spend from. Every time a client payment lands, transfer a fixed percentage **the same day**, before the money registers as yours. An interest-bearing account adds a small bonus for the discipline.

### 2. The right percentage

Start at **25–30%** of every receipt. Calibrate against reality: last year's assessment shows your effective rate — total tax over taxable income — and your set-aside should sit comfortably above it, because the percentage applies to receipts while tax applies to profit after deductions. Earning toward the upper brackets, or without meaningful deductions, push toward 35%. The test of the number is simple: did the account fully cover August and February without a top-up from anywhere else? If not, raise it.

### 3. The reserve is not a buffer

The account will, by December, hold more money than any other account you own. It is not the holiday fund, not the bridge loan for the late invoice, not "just until January". Every December raid reappears in late February with a 10% penalty attached if it forces a short payment. If you need a genuine emergency buffer — and freelancers do — build it as a separate second reserve, typically two to three months of expenses, so the tax account never has to be the lender.

## Preparing February in December

Beyond defending the reserve, early December is the right moment for a 30-minute pre-February review:

- **Refresh the year's estimate.** Ten months of actuals are in. Update the working paper from your August IRP6: income to date, contracted January–February work, realistic deductions. You'll see February's approximate number now, while there's still time to plan for it.
- **Check the reserve against that number.** Shortfall? You have twelve weeks of adjusted set-aside (or trimmed December spending) to close it — versus zero weeks if you discover it on 20 February.
- **Sequence the RA decision.** If your s11F plan involves a February top-up (up to 27.5% of taxable income, capped at R430,000), that cash also has to survive December. Decide now which rands are earmarked for the fund and which for SARS — both leave your account within days of each other in late February, and 28 February 2027 falling on a Sunday pulls the practical deadline to Friday the 26th.
- **Invoice everything finished, now.** Not for tax timing — for cash timing. Invoices entering client systems in early December have a chance of payment before the January freeze; mid-December invoices often don't. The accrual rule means the tax is coming regardless; the cash may as well come too.

## The Reframe That Makes It Stick

Freelancers who struggle with this cycle usually carry a quiet mental model where gross receipts are income and tax is a bill that ambushes them twice a year. The freelancers who don't struggle flipped the model: **a quarter to a third of every invoice was never yours.** You're briefly holding SARS's share on the way through, the way a VAT vendor holds output tax.

Adopt that frame and December loses its teeth: the client shutdowns still come, the invoices still drag, but the February payment is already sitting in an account with your name on it — earning interest until the day it's due.

## The Year That Sets the Pattern

Nobody goes freelance for the tax admin. But the freelancers who thrive treat year one's tax setup the way they treat their first client contracts: get the structure right once, and it quietly serves for a decade. Get it wrong and the correction — usually triggered by a penalty letter or a brutal first February — costs far more than the setup would have.

This is the year-one sequence, in the order it should happen. Most steps take minutes. All of them compound. And if you've arrived mid-year with none of them done — start at step one anyway; late beats never in every case below.

## Month One: The Foundations

### Separate the money

Open a dedicated tax savings account. From your **first client payment**, transfer 25–30% the day it lands. You don't yet know your effective rate — 25–30% is the defensible default, refined after your first assessment. This single habit is the difference between provisional deadlines being transfers and being crises; it cannot be retrofitted in deadline week.

A separate transactional account for the business generally (income in, expenses out) is the close second — it makes every later step, from bookkeeping to SARS's bank-statement reconciliations, dramatically cleaner.

### Confirm your SARS profile

You almost certainly have a tax number from employment. What changes: activate **provisional tax** under Tax Types on eFiling (freelance income makes you a provisional taxpayer by definition — the previous article on this site covers exactly who's in), and check your contact details, since everything SARS wants to tell you now arrives there.

### Start the records system on day one

One cloud folder per tax year, subfoldered: income, expenses, vehicle, home office, retirement-medical, SARS correspondence, working papers. Photograph every receipt the day it happens. If you'll claim vehicle travel, the logbook starts **now** — opening odometer, every business trip contemporaneously — because a reconstructed logbook is the most-rejected document in the deduction world, and the deduction lives or dies on it.

## Months Two and Three: Learn Your Deduction Landscape

Every rand of legitimate deduction reduces taxable income at your marginal rate, and freelancers — unburdened by the employee limitations in s23(m) — have the full s11(a) landscape: **home office** (if a space qualifies under the strict exclusive-use rules), **travel** (per that logbook), **equipment** (immediately under R7,000, via wear-and-tear schedules above it), professional fees, insurance, software, connectivity — the working tools of the trade.

Two deductions deserve deliberate setup rather than discovery:

- **Retirement (s11F)**: contributions deduct up to 27.5% of taxable income, capped at R430,000. Even a modest RA debit order started in month two builds the habit the February top-up strategy later optimises.
- **Medical credits**: scheme membership earns fixed monthly credits; the out-of-pocket receipts folder starts now for the year-end s6B arithmetic.

The monthly 15-minute close — reconcile income against bank, file the month's documents, update the logbook — is the entire maintenance burden if you start it early.

## August: Your First IRP6

By 31 August you'll submit your first provisional return: an estimate of the **full year's** taxable income, with roughly half the year's tax paid on it. The estimation method (annualise actuals, adjust for contracted work, deduct honestly, document the logic on one page) is covered in depth in this site's estimating guide — read it in July, not on the 30th.

First-year specifics worth knowing: you have no "basic amount" from prior assessments to fall back on, so your estimate is built from your actual numbers alone — which your records system has been quietly assembling since month one. And the payment won't hurt, because it's been sitting in the set-aside account since your first invoice. That's the system working.

## The February Bookend — and the Year's Full Calendar

Your first tax year closes on 28/29 February with the second, near-actual estimate (the one accuracy rules attach to), preceded ideally by a December cash-flow review and any RA top-up. Then filing season, months later, reconciles everything via your first ITR12 as a trader.

Diarise on day one: **31 August** (first IRP6), **end February** (second IRP6 + RA cut-off + closing odometer photo), **30 September of the following year** (voluntary top-up window), and filing season when announced. Four dates, reminders a month and a week out. Every article on this site hangs off one of them.

## The Five First-Year Mistakes

1. **Spending gross income** — the root of almost every provisional-tax horror story. The set-aside is the antidote, started on day one.
2. **No records until filing season** — deductions exist to the exact extent documents do; reconstruction recovers a fraction at many times the effort.
3. **Ignoring the August IRP6** because "SARS hasn't said anything" — the obligation arrives with the income, not the correspondence; discovering it via penalty letter is the expensive route.
4. **Copying employee tax logic** — your deductions, credits, and obligations run on trader rules; advice from salaried friends reliably misleads in both directions.
5. **Going it alone past your depth** — VAT-threshold proximity, entity questions, foreign income: these are the moments a professional pays for themselves. Everything else, this site and good records genuinely cover.

## The Standing Invitation

Freelance tax in South Africa is a system with four dates, two estimates, one return, and a set of deductions that reward nothing more exotic than contemporaneous paperwork. Set it up in month one, run the monthly close, read the seasonal guide when each date approaches — new ones publish here every ten days, tuned to exactly where the tax year stands — and year one's pattern becomes every year's routine.

Welcome to working for yourself. The tax part, it turns out, is learnable.

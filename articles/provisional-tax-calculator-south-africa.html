Provisional Tax Calculator South Africa 2026/27: How to Calculate What You Owe SARS

Use South Africa's provisional tax calculator for 2026/27 to estimate your IRP6 payments, apply deductions, and avoid SARS penalties — built for freelancers earning R400k to R1.5m.

June 01, 2026 11 min read Allan Lombard

What Is Provisional Tax in South Africa?

Provisional tax is not a separate tax — it's a payment method. SARS uses it to collect income tax in advance from taxpayers who earn income not automatically taxed through the PAYE system.

Employees have PAYE deducted by their employers every month. Freelancers, consultants, contractors, and anyone earning rental income or substantial investment returns do not have this automatic deduction. Provisional tax is how SARS ensures these taxpayers contribute throughout the year rather than in a single lump sum at filing time.

If you earn any of the following in the 2026/27 tax year, you are likely a provisional taxpayer:

  • Freelance or consulting fees
  • Contract income billed directly to clients
  • Rental income from property
  • Interest income above the annual exemption (R23,800 under 65, R34,500 from 65) — unless your only non-PAYE income is interest, dividends or rent that leaves taxable income at R30,000 or less
  • Business income from a sole proprietorship or partnership
  • Directors' fees from a company

How the South African Provisional Tax Calculator Works

Calculating provisional tax in South Africa follows a structured process. Understanding each step allows you to estimate accurately and apply every deduction you're entitled to.

Step 1: Estimate your total taxable income for the year

Add all anticipated income sources:

  • Freelance and consulting revenue
  • Contract income
  • Rental income received
  • Interest income (above the exemption)
  • Any other non-PAYE income

This is your gross income estimate.

Step 2: Subtract allowable deductions

Before applying tax tables, reduce your gross income by:

  • Retirement annuity contributions (s11F) — up to 27.5% of taxable income, capped at R430,000
  • Home office expenses (proportional)
  • Business travel (with logbook)
  • Professional expenses and subscriptions
  • Equipment depreciation

The result is your estimated taxable income.

Step 3: Apply the 2026/27 SARS tax tables

Apply the current year's tax tables to your estimated taxable income to calculate gross tax.

2026/27 Individual Tax Brackets:

  • R0 – R245,100: 18%
  • R245,101 – R383,100: R44,118 + 26% of excess above R245,100
  • R383,101 – R530,200: R79,998 + 31% of excess above R383,100
  • R530,201 – R695,800: R125,599 + 36% of excess above R530,200
  • R695,801 – R887,000: R185,215 + 39% of excess above R695,800
  • R887,001 – R1,878,600: R259,783 + 41% of excess above R887,000
  • Above R1,878,600: R666,339 + 45% of excess above R1,878,600

Step 4: Subtract rebates and credits

From your gross tax, deduct:

  • Primary rebate: R17,820 (all taxpayers)
  • Secondary rebate: an extra R9,765 (taxpayers aged 65 and above)
  • Tertiary rebate: an extra R3,249 (taxpayers aged 75 and above)
  • Medical aid tax credits: R376/month per main member and first dependant, R254/month per additional dependant
  • PAYE already paid (if you also receive a salary with PAYE deduction)

The result is your estimated annual tax liability.

Step 5: Calculate your provisional tax payment

Divide your estimated annual tax liability by two. This is your Period 1 or Period 2 provisional tax payment.

A Worked Example: Provisional Tax Calculator for a South African Contractor

Profile: Independent IT consultant, 35 years old, no dependants, medical aid (main member only), contributing R180,000 to a retirement annuity.

Income estimate for 2026/27:

  • Consulting fees: R850,000
  • Interest income: R12,000 (fully exempt — below the R23,800 annual interest exemption, so it adds nothing to taxable income)
  • Income for tax purposes: R850,000

Deductions:

  • s11F retirement annuity (27.5% of R850,000 = R233,750, within R430,000 cap): -R180,000 (actual contribution)
  • Home office (10% of R60,000 annual costs): -R6,000
  • Professional subscriptions and software: -R18,000
  • Business travel: -R12,000

Estimated taxable income: R634,000

Gross tax (2026/27 tables):

  • Tax on R530,200: R125,599
  • 36% on remaining R103,800 (R634,000 – R530,200): R37,368
  • Gross tax: R162,967

Deduct rebates and credits:

  • Primary rebate: -R17,820
  • Medical aid credit (R376 × 12): -R4,512

Annual tax liability: R140,635

Period 1 provisional tax payment: R70,318

Without the retirement annuity contribution, this taxpayer's liability would have been approximately R209,000 per year — a difference of about R68,000. That's the power of applying the s11F deduction before calculating provisional tax.

The Paragraph 20 Underestimation Penalty: What Your Provisional Tax Calculator Must Account For

South Africa's provisional tax system penalises significant underestimation of taxable income. Understanding this prevents costly surprises at year-end.

How the penalty works:

The test applies to your second-period estimate (Period 2, due end of February), measured against your actual taxable income once assessed.

The R1.8 million threshold (2026/27 onward):

If your actual taxable income for the year (as finally assessed) is R1.8 million or less, your second-period (February) estimate must be at least the lesser of 90% of actual taxable income and the basic amount shown on your IRP6. If actual taxable income is above R1.8 million, the estimate must be at least 80% of actual taxable income — the basic amount gives no protection. Fall short and SARS levies a penalty of 20% of the shortfall in tax: the difference between the normal tax on the required minimum estimate and the tax on your estimate.

Protecting yourself:

  • For Period 1 (August): SARS accepts the prior year's assessed income without penalty
  • For Period 2 (February), actual taxable income R1.8m or less: estimate at least the lesser of 90% of actual taxable income and the basic amount
  • For Period 2 (February), actual taxable income above R1.8m: estimate at least 80% of actual taxable income
  • The optional third payment (by 30 September) settles remaining liability at lower cost than the penalty

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate provisional tax in South Africa?

Calculate your estimated taxable income for the full year. Apply the current SARS tax tables to determine gross tax. Subtract your primary rebate, medical tax credits, and any PAYE already paid. Divide the result by two for your Period 1 or Period 2 payment.

Who needs to pay provisional tax in South Africa?

You must pay provisional tax if you earn income not subject to PAYE — including freelance income, consulting fees, rental income, or interest above the exemption threshold. Even part-time contractors earning alongside a salary may qualify.

What is the provisional tax deadline in South Africa?

The first provisional tax payment (Period 1) is due at the end of August each year. The second payment (Period 2) is due at the end of February. An optional third payment can be made by 30 September after year-end.

What happens if I underestimate my provisional tax in South Africa?

It depends on your February (second-period) estimate. If actual taxable income is R1.8 million or less, the estimate must be at least the lesser of 90% of actual taxable income and the basic amount; above R1.8 million it must be at least 80% of actual taxable income. Fall short and SARS levies a penalty of 20% of the shortfall in tax.

Can I use last year's income to calculate provisional tax?

Yes. SARS accepts the prior year's taxable income as a safe estimate for Period 1 without imposing an underestimation penalty. For Period 2, your estimate must be more accurate and reflect your actual year-to-date income.
